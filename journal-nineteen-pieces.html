A seasonless wardrobe in nineteen pieces

22 May 2026. Written in the workroom at 214 Sterling Road, Toronto. About 1,500 words.

Capsule wardrobe lists are usually written to sell you the contents of the list. This one is not, or at least not entirely: eleven of the nineteen items below are things we do not make and have no intention of making. We have included them because a list that only contains what one small company sells is not a wardrobe, it is a catalogue with a headline on it.

The constraint we set ourselves was this. Nineteen garments, excluding underwear and shoes, that will dress one adult through a full year in a climate with real winters and humid summers, for both work that does not require a suit and for ordinary life. Everything has to work with everything else, which in practice means a tight colour range and no strong patterns.

The principle: layers, not seasons

A seasonal wardrobe assumes you own summer clothes and winter clothes and swap between them. A seasonless one assumes you own one set of clothes and change how many of them you are wearing. The second approach needs fewer garments and it needs each garment to be thinner than you might expect.

The mistake almost everybody makes is buying one very warm thing instead of two moderately warm things. A single heavy coat has one setting. A mid-layer plus a shell has four: neither, one, the other, or both. In Toronto, where November can be plus twelve or minus eight in the same week, four settings is the difference between being comfortable and carrying your coat.

The nineteen

Next to skin, five pieces

  1. Three plain t-shirts. Heavyweight cotton, 200 gsm or above, in one colour. Thin t-shirts are a false economy; they lose their shape in the shoulders after twenty washes. We do not make these.
  2. A long-sleeved thermal top. Waffle or fine rib, cotton or merino. This is the piece that makes the rest of the wardrobe work in January, because it lets a mid-weight sweater do a heavy sweater's job. Ours is the waffle henley.
  3. A knitted polo or short-sleeved knit. The single most useful summer garment for anyone who wants to look slightly assembled without wearing a shirt. Ours is the cotton fisherman polo.

Shirts, two pieces

  1. An oxford shirt. White or pale blue, button-down collar, cut generously enough to wear open over a t-shirt. We do not make shirts and have no plans to; the market is well served and we would not add anything.
  2. A heavy overshirt. Wool or brushed cotton, sized to wear over a knit. It is the layer for a fifteen degree day when a jacket is too much. We do not make one, though we are asked for it more than anything else.

Knitwear, four pieces

  1. A fine merino crewneck. The workhorse. Thin enough to layer under everything, warm enough to be the only thing you have on indoors. Ours is the merino crewneck.
  2. A heavy wool sweater. One, not three. It should be so warm that you cannot wear it indoors comfortably, because that is what makes it useful outdoors. Ours is the fisherman rib.
  3. A cardigan. Because a sweater you can take off without disturbing your hair is a genuinely different garment from one you cannot. Ours is the lambswool cardigan.
  4. A knitted vest. The most underrated layer there is: it warms your core without adding bulk to your arms, which matters if you work with your hands or spend the day at a desk. Ours is the boiled wool vest.

Trousers, four pieces

  1. Dark denim. Rigid or lightly washed, straight or slightly tapered. We do not make trousers.
  2. A pair of wool trousers. Mid-grey. Warmer than denim, more formal than chinos, and comfortable in a way that surprises people who have only worn wool in a suit.
  3. Cotton work trousers or chinos. Sturdy, in a colour that hides dirt.
  4. One pair of shorts or lightweight trousers. For six weeks of the year. It is still worth owning.

Outerwear, three pieces

  1. A light unlined jacket. Chore coat, harrington or similar, worn from March to June and again from September to November. Ours is the cotton duck chore coat.
  2. A proper winter coat. One. It should be long enough to cover the heavy sweater and wind-tight. Ours is the waxed field parka, and the wool overcoat is the more formal alternative.
  3. A rain shell. Actually waterproof, packable, boring. Ours is the three-layer rain shell, and honestly, any decent one will do.

The nineteenth

  1. Something you like that does not fit the logic. A patterned shirt, a coloured knit, a jacket that is objectively impractical. Every rigorously built wardrobe we have seen without one of these becomes joyless within a year, and a joyless wardrobe gets abandoned, which defeats the entire point.

The accessories, which are not in the count

A wool hat, a scarf, gloves, a belt, three or four pairs of good socks, and one bag. None of these are garments in the sense above but the wardrobe does not function without them, and they are also the cheapest way to change how everything else looks. We make most of them; see accessories.

On colour

The reason a small wardrobe works is that everything goes with everything, and the reason everything goes with everything is a narrow colour range. Our suggestion, which is not a rule: two neutrals for the heavy pieces, in the navy, charcoal, olive, brown or ecru family, plus white or off-white for shirts and t-shirts, plus one accent that appears on no more than two items. Nineteen garments in a coherent range produce more genuinely wearable combinations than forty garments in a scattered one, which is a claim you can test in an afternoon with things you already own.

What this costs, and the honest caveat

Built entirely from things at our price level, nineteen pieces would run to something in the region of two and a half thousand dollars, which is a lot of money and which almost nobody has spare. So the practical version is replacement rather than acquisition: when something wears out, replace it with the best version of that slot you can afford, and stop buying anything that is not on the list. Assembled that way it takes three or four years and never costs more than a few hundred dollars in any one month.

The caveat we want to be clear about: a good wardrobe is not the same thing as an expensive one. The most useful item in the list above is a plain heavyweight t-shirt and the second is a decent rain shell. Neither of those has to be expensive, and neither of them is something we sell. If you take one thing from this piece, it should be the structure rather than the shopping.

A note on what we sell. Eight of the nineteen slots map to something we make, and we have linked those. The remaining eleven do not, and we would rather tell you that than pad the list. Nothing here is an affiliate link and we earn nothing if you buy a shirt somewhere else.
