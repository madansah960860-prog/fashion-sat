Why we do not have a sale, and what we do instead

3 July 2026. Written in the workroom at 214 Sterling Road, Toronto. About 1,400 words.

Every year around the end of June and again in November, we get a wave of emails asking when the sale is. The answer is that there is not one and there never has been. That answer annoys people, reasonably, because everyone else has one and because being told no is irritating. So here is the reasoning written out properly, including the parts that do not flatter us.

Markdown is priced in before anything is made

Start with how a conventional clothing business plans a season. Before a single garment is cut, the buyer builds a plan that assumes a percentage of the range will not sell at full price. In department store wholesale that assumption is often forty to sixty per cent of units. The plan is built around a blended margin: some units sell at full price, some at thirty per cent off, some at fifty, some go to an outlet, and the average of all that has to land above the cost of goods with enough left over to run the business.

The consequence is arithmetic rather than ethics. If you know in advance that half your stock will sell at half price, the full price has to be set high enough that the blended average still works. The full-price customer is therefore paying for the markdown customer. That is not a scandal, it is just what the model requires, but it is worth naming clearly: in a business that discounts predictably, the sticker price is not really the price. It is an opening position.

Once you have said that out loud, a second thing follows. The reason a garment in that system carries a gross margin of sixty or seventy per cent is not that somebody is being greedy. It is that the margin has to absorb the markdown, the returns, the unsold, the wholesale discount to the retailer, and the cost of the season's photography and marketing, which has to be re-made from scratch twice a year because the range changes twice a year.

What we do differently, and what it costs us

We make one range. It does not change in February and it does not change in August. There is no season to clear, because there is no season. When we run out of a lot of cloth we say so, and when the next lot arrives we make more of the same thing. The three confirmed runs for the coming year are variations in colour, not new products.

Because we never plan to discount, we do not need a margin that absorbs discounting. Our retained margin is about eight per cent, and the full cost breakdown is published on our story page. Eight per cent is thin. It is thin enough that a bad quarter is genuinely a problem, and it is the honest reason we cannot run a promotion even if we wanted to.

The arithmetic of a discount on a thin margin

This is the part people find surprising, so here it is with numbers.

Take the merino crewneck at 165 USD. Materials and labour on that garment come to roughly 89 USD. Shipping, fulfilment, card fees, rent, power and the repairs programme come to roughly another 45 USD once allocated. That leaves about 31 USD before tax, and about 13 USD after it.

Now discount it by twenty per cent, which is a modest sale by any standard. That is 33 USD off. The 13 USD becomes minus 20 USD. We would be paying for the privilege of selling you a sweater.

To make a twenty per cent discount work we would need the pre-discount margin to be roughly three times what it is, which means the sweater would need to cost around 210 USD at full price. Which is the whole point: a business that discounts has already charged you for the discount.

The objections, which are fair

"You are just describing a higher price with better manners." Partly, yes. The claim is not that our prices are low. A 165 USD sweater is not cheap. The claim is narrower: the number on the tag is the number, it is the same number in June and in December, and nobody who bought one last week is going to feel foolish next week.

"Sales make clothes accessible to people who cannot pay full price." This is the strongest objection and we do not have a complete answer to it. What we do have is a set of partial ones. Our range starts at 18 USD and there are six items under 100 USD. We run a seconds list for garments with real cosmetic faults, sold at a fixed 40 per cent less with the fault photographed and described. And our repairs programme means an existing garment can be kept going rather than replaced, which is the cheapest clothing there is. None of that is the same as a sale, and we are not going to pretend it fully closes the gap.

"Everybody says this and then runs a Black Friday sale." True, and there is no way to prove a negative in advance. What we can say is that we have been trading since 2019 and have not discounted a single garment in that time, that our margin structure makes it arithmetically impossible without raising prices first, and that if we ever do run one we will say plainly on this page that the argument above no longer applies.

What we do instead

  • The price is the price. Same number all year, everywhere, including at our stockists, who agree not to discount as a condition of carrying the range.
  • Seconds, honestly described. Genuine faults, photographed close up, at a fixed reduction. Not a euphemism for old stock.
  • Free repairs for life. Materials and postage only, no labour charge, for anything we made.
  • Free returns and free exchanges. Which sounds like a perk and is really a way of removing the risk of buying at full price.
  • A referral credit. 20 USD for the person you refer on their first order over 120 USD, and 20 USD for you once their return window closes. We wait for the window because otherwise we would be paying credits on returns.
  • Take-back credit. 25 USD for a worn-out garment we made, if we can use the material.

The uncomfortable part

Not discounting is easier for us than it sounds, because our range is small and seasonless. A business with two hundred styles and a spring delivery genuinely cannot do this; by August they are sitting on stock that has to move to free up cash and warehouse space, and no amount of principle changes that. We are not better than those businesses. We have a structurally simpler problem, and we designed the business that way on purpose because we had all watched the other version from the inside.

The honest summary is this. We do not run sales because we cannot afford to, and we cannot afford to because we chose not to build the margin that would let us. What you get in exchange is a price that means what it says. Whether that is a good trade is genuinely up to you, and if the answer is no, we would rather you knew that before you ordered than after.
