What waxed cotton actually does, and what it does not do

14 August 2026. Written in the workroom at 214 Sterling Road, Toronto. About 1,500 words.

We sell one waxed garment, the field parka, and it generates more email than everything else in the range put together. Almost all of it is one of four questions: is it waterproof, why can I not wash it, how do I re-wax it, and why does it smell like that. This is the long answer to all four, written once so we can stop writing four different short ones.

What waxed cotton is

Waxed cotton is exactly what the name says: a tightly woven cotton cloth with wax driven into it. The technique is old, and it comes from sailing. Crews in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries noticed that wet sails held wind better than dry ones, and started treating worn sailcloth with fish oil and grease to keep it heavy and wind-tight. The offcuts became capes. That is genuinely the lineage; a waxed jacket is a descendant of a sail.

The fish oil stage lasted a long time and had obvious problems. It went rancid, it stained everything it touched, and it yellowed badly. Paraffin arrived in the nineteenth century as a by-product of the petroleum industry and solved most of that, and by the 1930s the modern formulation existed: a mix of paraffin wax and a small amount of natural oil, worked into a densely woven cotton at heat.

The cloth in our parka is a 10 oz organic cotton woven and waxed at a mill in Lancashire that has been doing this since 1864. Ten ounces is the weight of a square yard of the finished cloth. For comparison, a heavy denim jacket is around 12.5 oz and a shirt is 3 to 4 oz.

How it keeps water out, and why that is limited

Two things are happening. First, the wax coats each cotton fibre and makes it hydrophobic, so water beads and rolls rather than soaking in. Second, and more importantly, cotton swells when it gets wet. As the fibres take on moisture they expand and close the gaps in the weave, so a waxed cotton jacket becomes progressively more wind and water tight in the first minutes of rain. It is a self-sealing cloth.

That is also its ceiling. There is no membrane. There is no coating on the inside. Once the fibres are fully saturated and the wax layer on the outside has been mechanically disturbed by hours of rain hitting it, water starts getting through, and it does so first at the shoulders and the top of the sleeves where the rain hits hardest and where the cloth flexes most.

In practice, from what customers tell us and from what we have measured standing in the lane behind the studio with a hose: a well-waxed parka keeps you dry through two to three hours of steady rain, and considerably less in driving rain. After that you get damp shoulders. It is water resistant. It is not waterproof, and we will not print the word waterproof on it.

What it is genuinely excellent at is wind. A waxed cotton shell is very nearly windproof, better than most soft shells, and it is quiet, which nothing with a membrane is. It also breathes far more than a laminate, so you do not get the clammy feeling of a rain shell on a mild damp day. If you want something for sustained downpours, buy an actual shell; ours is the three-layer rain shell and it is made to order.

Dry finish and hot wax are not the same thing

There are two broad families of waxed cotton, and the difference matters a great deal for how a jacket behaves in ordinary life.

Traditional or hot wax is heavily impregnated, tacky to the touch, noticeably shiny, and it transfers. Sit on a light-coloured car seat in a hot-waxed jacket in August and you will find out. It is the most water resistant version and it is what you want if you spend the day outdoors in weather.

Dry finish wax, which is what we use, has less wax and it is worked further into the fibre rather than sitting on the surface. It feels like a firm cotton canvas rather than an oilcloth. It does not transfer onto furniture, it does not smell as strongly, and it looks matte rather than shiny. It is somewhat less water resistant than hot wax, and it needs re-waxing slightly more often.

We chose dry finish because the parka is a jacket people wear to work and to the shops, not only up a hillside, and a coat you cannot sit down in wearing pale trousers is a coat that stays on the hook.

The smell

New waxed cotton smells. It is a slightly oily, slightly sweet, faintly industrial smell, and it comes from the paraffin and the oil in the blend. It is strongest in the first fortnight, strongest again on a hot day, and it fades substantially over the first season. Hanging the jacket outdoors in a breeze for a couple of days speeds this up considerably. Some people like it. Some people return the jacket over it, which is entirely fair, and if that is you, the return is free.

The three things that ruin a waxed jacket

  1. Washing it. Detergent is designed to remove oil. Wax is oil. A single machine wash will take most of the wax out of the cloth and what is left after that is a slightly stiff, no longer water resistant cotton jacket. It cannot be undone with one re-waxing; it takes two or three, and the cloth never quite recovers its original hand.
  2. Dry cleaning it. Worse than washing. Dry cleaning solvent is extremely effective at stripping wax, and the heat in the press finishes the job.
  3. Drying it with heat. Hanging a soaked waxed jacket over a radiator or in front of a wood stove melts the wax, which then migrates and pools. You end up with dry patches at the shoulders and greasy patches at the hem. Hang it somewhere cool with air moving, and be patient.

Everything else is survivable. Mud comes off with cold water and a sponge. Salt stains from a Toronto winter come off with cold water and a soft brush. Scuffs are the point.

How to re-wax a jacket at your kitchen table

Once a season, at the end of winter, or whenever water stops beading on the shoulders. It takes about an hour, most of which is waiting.

  1. Clean the jacket first, with cold water and a sponge. Waxing over dirt seals the dirt in.
  2. Let it dry completely. Overnight, minimum.
  3. Stand the tin of wax in a bowl of hot water for ten minutes until it softens to about the consistency of soft butter. Do not heat it in a pan on a hob; paraffin is flammable and there is no reason to take the risk.
  4. Work the wax in with a lint-free cloth in small circles. Do the seams, the shoulders and the elbows twice. Do not miss the underside of the collar or the inside of the cuffs.
  5. Use less than you think you need. A jacket takes about half a tin. The most common mistake is caking it on.
  6. Hang it somewhere warm, around 25 degrees, for a few hours. A hairdryer on a low setting, kept moving, helps the wax draw into the weave and evens out the finish.
  7. Leave it overnight before wearing. It will look patchy at first and even out over a week.

We do not sell wax. Any pure paraffin dressing sold for waxed cotton works, and they are all much the same. We will do it for you if you would rather: send the jacket to the studio and it costs roughly 20 USD in materials and return postage, with no labour charge, like all of our repairs.

What a waxed jacket looks like after five years

Lighter at every fold and seam, because that is where the wax breaks first. Noticeably scuffed at the elbows and at the front hip where you rest your hands. Darker where it has been handled, particularly the collar and the pocket flaps. If it has been re-waxed regularly, still water resistant. If it has not, no longer water resistant but still a very good windproof jacket.

It will not look new, and nothing you do will make it look new. That is the actual argument for waxed cotton and it is worth being clear-eyed about it: you are buying a cloth that records what you did in it. Some people find that appealing. Some people find it looks scruffy. Both reactions are correct and only one of them should be buying the jacket.

Sources. Cloth specification and finishing method supplied by our mill in Lancashire.
