Gauge, and why it is the number that matters

Gauge is the number of needles per inch on the knitting machine. A low number means fat yarn and a heavy fabric; a high number means fine yarn and a light one. It tells you more about how a sweater will actually behave than the word on the label does, so we print it on every product.

Washing wool without ruining it

Almost every ruined sweater is ruined by one of three things: heat, agitation, or hanging it up wet. Wool fibres have scales. Heat and movement make those scales lock together, which is felting, and felting is permanent.

  1. Fill a basin with cold water. Cold means cold from the tap, not lukewarm.
  2. Add a capful of a detergent made for wool. Ordinary detergent contains enzymes that digest protein, and wool is protein.
  3. Submerge the sweater and leave it. Do not rub, do not swirl, do not wring. Ten minutes is plenty.
  4. Drain, press the water out against the side of the basin, then roll the sweater in a towel and stand on it.
  5. Dry it flat, in shape, away from radiators and direct sun. On a rack is ideal. On a hanger it will grow four inches and stay that way.

A front-loading machine with a genuine wool cycle, cold, at 400 rpm or less, is fine for the merino and the mock neck. It is not fine for the Shetland or the fisherman rib, both of which are loosely spun and will felt.

Pilling is not a fault

All wool pills where it rubs: under the arms, at the sides, across a seatbelt line. Short fibres work their way to the surface and tangle. It is most noticeable in the first month and then it stops. Take the pills off with a comb or a cheap battery sweater shaver, and do not pull them, because pulling takes a length of yarn with it. We include a small comb with every knit.

Moth holes and elbow wear are repairable, and we will do it. Send an email with a photograph and we will quote materials and postage, with no labour charge, for any knit we made.
