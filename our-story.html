Our Story

Where this started

In March 2019, three of us rented Unit 6 at 214 Sterling Road in Toronto, a room with two windows, a concrete floor and a freight lift that worked most of the time. Two of us had spent the previous decade doing production for other clothing companies, mostly the unglamorous end of it: chasing a mill in Portugal about a delayed lot, arguing about a half-inch of ease, sitting in a warehouse counting returns. One of us had been a bookbinder and knew nothing about clothes but a great deal about making a small number of physical objects properly.

What we had all seen, from the inside, was how much of a garment's retail price has nothing to do with the garment. A coat that costs forty-two dollars to make lands on a shop floor at three hundred and fifty. Somewhere in that gap sit two wholesale margins, a season's worth of photography, an agency, a showroom in a city none of the customers live in, and a markdown allowance built in from the start because half of it will not sell at full price.

We did not think we could fix any of that at scale. We did think that if you sold directly, made fourteen things instead of two hundred, and stopped paying for the parts of the business that exist to make people feel a particular way, you could sell a genuinely good coat for less and still pay everyone properly. That is the whole thesis. Seven years in, it has held up.

Who works here

Eleven people, all in Ontario. Four cut and sew in the Sterling Road unit. Two run the knitting and linking in a workshop we share in the east end. One does nothing but repairs and re-waxing, which turned out to be a full-time job by year three. Two pack and ship. One does the books. One answers the email, and if you write to us it is a person who has actually held the garment who writes back.

Everyone is paid an hourly rate at or above the Ontario living wage as calculated by the Ontario Living Wage Network for the Greater Toronto Area, reviewed each November. Nobody here is paid per piece. Piece rates are how you get a fast seam and a bad one.

What we do not do

  • Seasons. There is no autumn/winter and no spring/summer. The capsule is the capsule. It is available in July and it is available in January.
  • Sales. We have never had one and do not intend to. The reasoning is set out at length in this journal entry.
  • Trend forecasting. We do not buy a forecast, we do not attend the fabric fairs looking for a colour story, and we do not have a creative director.
  • Influencer seeding. Nobody has been sent a free coat in exchange for a post. If you see our clothes on somebody, they bought them.
  • Fake urgency. No countdown timers, no "only two left", no invented waitlists. Our release dates are production dates.
  • Reviews we cannot verify. There are no star ratings on this site. When we can build a system that only accepts a review from a verified order, we will add one, and not before.

An eight per cent retained margin is thin for retail. It works because we do not carry markdown risk: we do not produce a garment we expect to discount, so we do not need a forty per cent gross margin to absorb one. It also means we are not in a position to run promotions, and that is a fact about the business rather than a stance we adopted for its own sake.

Why the site looks like this

The website is the second thing people ask about. The short answer is that it is a document, and a document is what a shop like this actually needs.

The typefaces are the ones already sitting on your device, a serif for headings against a humanist sans for reading. No font files download, so nothing reflows halfway through your first sentence. There is one accent colour, pulled from the rust in the waxed cotton, and it is only allowed to appear on things that carry meaning: a price, a link, the button you are meant to press. Everything else is paper, ink and a single hairline rule.

Product photographs are cropped to one consistent ratio. That is a real change from how we used to publish them, and we argued about it: an inconsistent crop is more honest to the frame, but a consistent one lets you compare twenty-six garments instead of looking at twenty-six pictures. The catalogue won.

Motion is limited to things that report a state. A block settles as you reach it, a card lifts under the cursor, the menu slides rather than teleports. All of it is switched off, not merely shortened, if your device asks for reduced motion. Nothing auto-plays, nothing pops up, nothing interrupts.

The practical outcome is a page that weighs a few tens of kilobytes before images, opens on a slow connection in northern Ontario about as quickly as it does on fibre downtown, works properly at 320 pixels wide on an old iPhone, follows your system into dark mode, and can be read end to end by a screen reader without anything in the way. We would rather spend the budget on the fifth pocket bag than on a video header.

There is a longer argument about all of this in the journal.

A short chronology

Plaintext Supply Co., 2019 to 2026
YearWhat happened
2019Unit 6 rented in March. Four garments: chore coat, henley, merino crew, tote. First order shipped 11 June to an address in Hamilton.
2020Stopped production for eleven weeks. Made and gave away 2,400 cotton masks with the cloth we could not use. Nearly closed.
2021Knitting moved from a contractor to the shared workshop in the east end. Fisherman rib added.
2022Field parka introduced. First waxed cloth lot from Lancashire. First year the business paid everybody for twelve months without a gap.
2023Repairs became a full-time role. First three stockists, in Montreal, Vancouver and Portland.
2024Undyed programme started: Shetland crew and the rib sweater moved to no dye bath at all.
2025Boiled wool vest introduced to use surplus yarn. Range settled at 26 pieces.
2026Made-to-order line opened with the rain shell. Studio opened to visitors Tuesday to Saturday.

Come and see it

The studio is open to anyone Tuesday to Saturday, 11:00 to 18:00 Eastern. Everything in the range is on a rail and you can try it on. There is no appointment system, no host, and nobody will follow you around. If you want to see a garment turned inside out, ask, and somebody will do it.
