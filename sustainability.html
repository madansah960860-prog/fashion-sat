Sustainability and Materials

Making clothes has an environmental cost. Making fewer of them, out of better material, and keeping them in use longer, lowers that cost. It does not remove it. Nothing on this page is a claim that buying from us is good for the planet; the honest framing is that it is less bad than the alternative, and we can show our working.

We do not use the words eco, green, conscious or planet-friendly anywhere on this site, because none of them mean anything specific. What follows instead is a list of fibres, where they come from, which third party has audited what, and a section at the end on the problems we have not solved.

Where a row says "no certification", that is deliberate. It would be easy to imply one by writing vaguely. Certification costs money, small spinners often cannot carry it, and the absence of a logo is not the same as a problem. It is, however, an absence, and you should be told about it rather than left to assume.

Undyed, and what that actually saves

Two of our knits have no dye bath at all: the Shetland crew and the fisherman rib. The colour is the fleece.

Dyeing a wool sweater uses roughly 40 to 60 litres of water and heats it to around 95 degrees for an hour. Skipping it saves that water and that energy per garment, and it removes the effluent load entirely. It also means the shade varies between lots, which some people dislike and which we cannot fix without reintroducing the dye bath.

We are not going to claim a total figure for what this saves across the business, because we would have to guess at the mill's process water recovery rate and we do not have that number. A range of forty to sixty litres per garment is what the spinner told us, and that is the extent of what we can substantiate.

Deadstock and surplus

The boiled wool vest exists because we were disposing of roughly ninety kilos of leftover yarn a year, in quantities too small to make a full run of anything. Knitting it loose and then boiling it produces a dense fabric that tolerates mixed lots, because fulling hides minor differences in yarn. That is why the vest's colour varies between production runs and why we tell you so on the product page rather than in a footnote.

The card holder is cut from belt offcuts for the same reason. A belt strap is cut from the strongest part of a hide along the backbone; what is left at the edges is perfectly good leather in awkward shapes.

Repairs

One person here does nothing but repairs. We will fix anything we made, for as long as we can source the parts, at cost of materials plus postage and with no labour charge. That covers:

  • Moth holes and worn elbows in knitwear, darned in matching yarn.
  • Zip replacement in outerwear.
  • Re-waxing the field parka. Roughly 20 USD in materials and postage.
  • Buttons, seams, pocket bags and cuff replacement.
  • Belt shortening and buckle replacement.
  • Handle and base repairs on canvas bags.

Email a photograph to repairs@plaintextsupply.ca. You will get a quote before you post anything, and a realistic turnaround, which in practice is two to four weeks and longer in January.

Take-back

If a garment we made is genuinely finished, send it back rather than binning it. We will pay the postage within Canada and the United States. Wool goes back to the shared workshop to be shredded into fill for the liner jacket; cotton is cut down into the cloths we use in the studio; leather is kept for card holders. You get a 25 USD credit for anything we can use.

We are not going to describe this as closed-loop, because it is not. A proportion of what comes back is unusable and goes to textile waste like anything else. In 2025 we received 214 garments and were able to use 163 of them.

Packaging

  • Garments ship in an unbleached cotton pouch, which is reusable and is the packaging, not an extra.
  • Outer mailers are recycled and recyclable kraft paper, sealed with paper tape. No plastic mailers, no polybags, no tissue, no stickers.
  • The invoice is printed on the inside of the mailer flap rather than on a separate sheet.
  • There is no branded box, no ribbon, no card set, no sample sachet.

Shipping emissions

We ship from Toronto by ground within Canada and the United States, which is a fraction of the emissions of air freight but slower, which is why our transit times on the shipping page are longer than a large retailer's. We do not offer next-day delivery and do not intend to. International orders go by air because there is no realistic alternative, and we say that plainly rather than offsetting it and calling it neutral.

What we have not solved

This is the section most companies leave out.

  • The denim is conventional cotton. The mill in Okayama does not offer an organic version of the cloth we want, and we have not been willing to change the cloth. That is a straightforward trade-off in favour of the garment.
  • Canvas is Better Cotton, not organic. We could not find an 18 oz GOTS canvas at a price that kept the tote under 100 USD. We are still looking.
  • Microfibres. The anorak, the rain shell and the liner jacket shed synthetic microfibre when washed. Recycled polyester sheds just like virgin polyester does. A filter bag helps; it does not fix it.
  • We do not know our full footprint. We have never had a lifecycle assessment done. They cost more than we can currently justify, and quoting a number we have not measured would be worse than admitting the gap.
  • Alpaca has no standard. There is no equivalent of the Responsible Wool Standard for alpaca. We visit the co-operative every two years and that is the extent of our verification.
  • Free returns cost carbon. Our returns policy encourages ordering two sizes. That is better for fit and worse for emissions, and we have not found a way to have both.
